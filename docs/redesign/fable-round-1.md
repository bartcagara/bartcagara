## e. A first-name field on /brief

**Recommendation: don't test it and don't add it.**
- About 100 visitors and 13 signups since September can't settle an A/B test. A real difference would take many months to show, and until then every reading is noise.
- An extra field asks more of the visitor before he gets anything. On a page this small, losing even two or three signups is a large share. (That an extra field lowers signups is general experience with forms. It is not measured on this page.)
- What a first name buys is small. The emails that greet by name already fall back to "man" (`03_content/newsletter/kit-design.md`, the Bare layout), which is how Bart talks.
- The quiz gate already asks for first name and email, at the moment he wants something in return. Men who come in that way arrive with a name. The booking form collects it for everyone who books.

If Bart wants names for /brief signups anyway, the cheaper way is the one already used on 8 Nov: set the first name by hand where the address makes it obvious (`03_content/newsletter/kit-automation-checklist.md`, line 57).

## f. Design direction (words only)

**What "clean, premium, elevated, very confident, not a generic fitness website" means for this man.** He runs a company and buys expensive services. He decides in seconds whether a page was made by someone at his level.
- **Clean:** he never has to work out where to look. One thing per screen.
- **Premium:** restraint. Few elements, real things (Bart with his son, a real message, a real photo), nothing stock, nothing decorative.
- **Elevated:** the same brand with more care. Even spacing, type sizes that step down in a clear order, pictures cropped on purpose.
- **Very confident:** few words at a size he can read, one button, proof stated flat. No urgency, no countdown, no pleading. Bart: "overwriting smells of desperation." The same holds for a layout that shouts.
- **Not a generic fitness site:** no gym stock photos, no neon or gradients, no wall of before-and-afters at the top, no badge rows, no macros. The first picture he sees is a father, not a torso.

Both openings keep what Bart has ruled on: Inter only, the current colours, sharp edges, subtle offset shadows, no display serif, no dark premium concept, no giant all-caps headline over small grey text, the dark message screenshots as the page's contrast.

**Opening A: the current look, elevated**
- Same sections and the same blocks, with fewer of them on each screen.
- Three clear type sizes: the headline, a subhead in near-full-strength colour at a size he reads without squinting, and body. The subhead tier is what Bart found missing before.
- Headlines in sentence case. Capitals only on small labels. Section titles like "SHIRT OFF RUINS THE WHOLE THING" are where the page shouts today.
- The offset shadow only on things he can press and on real artefacts (the button, a screenshot, a photo). When every box has one, none stands out.
- The blue accent once per screen: the button and the highlighted words in the headline.
- Hero: words on the left, the photo of Bart with his son on the right, the proof line with a face under the button. On a phone the headline, subhead, button and proof line all fit before the first scroll.
- More white space between sections, and each section ends on its one bold line (the rule in `brand-voice.md`).

**Opening B: my free proposal, "a letter with the evidence attached"**
- The page reads as one man writing to one man. The argument sections (this is you, why it kept ending, the six months, the call) sit in a single narrow reading column, about the width of an email, in sentence-length lines with no boxes around them.
- The proof breaks out of the column at full width, as the real things: the dark message screenshots, the photos, a client's sentence set large with his name and face. The rhythm down the page is letter, evidence, letter, evidence.
- No section badges and no brochure titles. Each section opens with a plain sentence that works as its heading, so a man who reads only the headings still gets the argument.
- The hero is words first: the label, the headline, the subhead, the button, the proof line. Bart's photo is small and near his name, the way the newsletter signs off. The blue line he liked in the emails ("elegant distance") separates the hero from the letter.
- The same vocabulary as today: Inter, the same blues, sharp corners, the offset shadow on the button and on each piece of evidence. So /brief, /book and /quiz stay one family and take the same treatment: one column, one artefact, one button. The quiz results and the Letter emails already read this way.
- Why I think it fits him: the page's job is belief, and for this reader belief comes from being spoken to plainly and shown real things. A grid of features is what every fitness site has. A letter with evidence is what a confident man sends.
- Its risk: a long column of text can turn into a wall. It only works if each letter section stays short and an artefact arrives before he tires. It needs checking at phone width first, since that is where most LinkedIn clicks land.

Which to pick is a question for the two built openings, not for me now. I'd expect A to feel safer and B to be the one he remembers.
